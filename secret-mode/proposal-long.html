The problem, as you described it

Secret Mode is building faster than its planning can keep up with. Two things follow from that, and you named both.

There is no coherent plan for the catalogue. Titles you want to do more with — Chained Together on console, Asia, others — have no value-creation plan behind them. There’s no tiering, so no shared language for what a tentpole is versus what catalogue is. And that isn’t a documentation gap: you can’t tier a portfolio before you know what each title could become. The growth plans have to come first.

And nobody holds the pen. Commercial signs the deals and secures the platform commitments. Marketing takes titles to market. The franchise plan that should sit between them — what this IP becomes, in what order, funded how — belongs to neither, so it doesn’t get written.

Underneath both: eleven launches of note currently sitting in March 2027, four live-service titles under a single owner, and a marketing group of 25–30 running on community feedback with no analyst and no telemetry.

What this engagement is

Someone picks up the pen, produces a v1.0 the whole business believes in, and then makes themselves unnecessary.

Three phases over six months, with programme management running throughout.

The work
Phase 1 · Weeks 1–6

Establish the picture

You can’t plan a portfolio you can’t see. This phase produces the facts that currently live in several heads and no documents.

  • Catalogue audit. Every title: rights position (owned outright / publishing rights / rev-share), what reverts and when, what’s already been granted away, contribution, current state.
  • Data and evidence audit. What feeds exist, what’s contractually available from dev partners, and what’s simply unplumbed. The output is a specification, not a wish.
  • The March 2027 map. All eleven launches on one page with their real dependencies and real constraints, separating what’s fixed from what merely feels fixed.
Immediate output: a de-conflicted launch calendar. This is the first thing that changes, because it’s the thing hurting now.
Phase 2 · Weeks 6–16

Portfolio Map v1.0

The destination: what success looks like in January 2028, and the route to it.

  • Value-creation plan per priority IP. What each title could become, the ranked moves, and the evidence behind each. Chained Together console and Asia are the obvious first two.
  • Tiering, derived not declared. Once each title has a growth thesis, Tier A / B / C assigns itself — and so does the resourcing each tier earns.
  • Sequenced against real capacity, including Secret Code’s ramp and the Lab42 handover on Chained Together.
  • The Jan 2028 picture — roughly fifteen months out. Far enough to complete real moves, close enough to plan against honestly.

Worth being precise about what that horizon can and can’t contain, because it sets expectations for everyone who reads the map:

By Jan 2028Meaning
ShippedMoves completed and earning — platform and territory expansion, live content
ProvedA playbook applied more than once, with measured results behind it
In flight, de-riskedBigger bets committed and in production, with the evidence that justified them

A sequel, for instance, is a Jan 2028 in-flight item, not a shipped one. Saying that up front is more useful than discovering it in month twelve.

One deliberate sequencing note: the map defines the portfolio state at Jan 2028 before it attaches financial targets to it. Until the catalogue audit shows what each title actually contributes, any revenue number would be invented rather than derived — and a target nobody can trace back to a base is a target nobody defends six months later.

Built with Marketing, Commercial and Publishing as co-authors, not delivered to them. A plan the business believes in is one it helped write; anything else is a document that gets praised once and ignored thereafter.

Phase 3 · Weeks 16–26

Make it run without me

A map is worthless if refreshing it requires me.

  • Decision rights. Who owns the franchise plan for each IP, who signs what, and how Marketing, Commercial, Publishing and Secret Code interlock. Written down.
  • The operating cadence. A portfolio review rhythm that actually runs — agenda, inputs, templates, owners — so the map stays current rather than going stale in a drive.
  • The evidence layer. Analyst role scoped and, ideally, filled; the first telemetry pipes specified and in progress.
  • The permanent pen. Role definition for whoever owns this after me, and support recruiting them.
Running throughout

Programme management

The portfolio map is the destination. The launch calendar is what stops you falling over on the way there. Both run from week one — the calendar is continuous, holistic oversight across the whole slate, not a Phase 1 deliverable that then sits still.

Two things worth saying now

Hire an analyst before you spend much more on advice

A 25–30 person marketing group with no analyst is the cheapest gap in the business to close, and it’s the precondition for every recommendation I make being evidence-led rather than merely well-argued. I’d rather tell you that in week one than bill you for the workaround.

Decide your rule on licensed work before Galactic Racer launches

If it goes as well as you expect, the pull toward more top-tier licensed work will intensify — and that work buys reputation and cash flow while consuming exactly the capacity your owned IP needs to build equity value. That’s not an argument against taking it. It’s an argument for knowing, in advance and in writing, what you’d say yes to and what you wouldn’t.
