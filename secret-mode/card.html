In-room card

What are the IPs actually worth?

Emona will want to sell. That needs an equity story built on IP Secret Mode owns — not on a licence. Which moves, in what order, and can the team run them.

Open with

“Player trust is the leading indicator. Enterprise value is the lagging one.”

Diligence reads revenue — where the asset has been. Sentiment tells you where it’s going, two to four quarters ahead. So you see erosion before it prices in, and you can evidence value creation before it lands in the P&L. That second one is the hardest thing to do in a sale process.

The frame

Galactic Racer has to go well, and going well buys reputation, licensor relationships and proof of delivery — all of which lift the multiple. It doesn’t lift the asset base: when the licence ends the revenue ends, and nothing transfers to an acquirer.

So the whole show is the owned IP. And the plan can’t be written until the rights position is on one page — owned outright vs publishing rights, what reverts and when, what’s already been granted away. Offer to be the person who writes that page.

The principle

Sequence by evidence generated per pound spent — not revenue per pound spent.

They’re building a plan a buyer has to believe. A proven, repeatable playbook earns a premium; an unproven roadmap gets discounted. So the early moves should be cheap, fast, low-risk and proof-generating — they’re what make the expensive later moves fundable.

The ladder
01

Harvest what’s proven

0–9 MO · LOW RISK

Console and platform, territory partners, edition and pricing. Monetises demand that already exists without touching the game or the player promise.

Console for Chained Together is the standout — 10M+ owners at $5 on PC, console audiences pay more for couch co-op, and Sheffield was explicitly built to do ports. An eastern partner is Tier 1 too: the partner does the work, and it proves the IP travels.

⚠️ Every right you grant is a right the buyer doesn’t get. A five-year exclusive signed eighteen months before a sale can cost more in valuation than it earns in fees. Short terms, reversion, change-of-control.
02

Prove recurring revenue

6–18 MO · THE MULTIPLE

Content, cosmetics, seasonal cadence. This is what Sheffield exists for, and it’s the biggest single driver of how a buyer values the business — recurring revenue at a known content cost is an annuity; launch spikes are not.

Chained Together is the candidate and the biggest risk in the portfolio: every monetisation option renegotiates a deal 10 million people think closed at five dollars. Worth de-risking with evidence — and Lab42’s roadmap is being written right now.

03

The big bet

18 MO+ · HIGH VARIANCE

A sequel — the only move that resets an IP’s clock and turns a decaying asset into a franchise. Also the one that eats the most capacity and is most likely to land after the window closes.

An announced-but-unshipped sequel is worth far less at exit than a shipped, growing live title. Only commit if it ships and shows traction inside the window — on the most defensibly owned IP, funded by Tiers 1 and 2.

For a two-to-three year window, the sequel is usually the wrong first move.

It’s the most exciting option and it feels the most like value creation, which is why teams reach for it. But ports and territories convert proven demand into cash and evidence in months, live content proves the annuity, and the sequel is what those two earn you the right to fund. Run it the other way round and you spend the whole window on the highest-variance bet with nothing proved behind it.

Two things diligence will find

The Backrooms is shared creepypasta. Competing Backrooms games are already on Steam. Secret Mode owns its execution — title, levels, brand, community, 46k reviews — but not the concept, and a buyer’s IP counsel will ask. Its moat is audience and execution, not exclusivity. Don’t put the most expensive bet on the least defensible IP.

A throughput-measured content studio depreciates the asset it was built to grow. The live gripe that Backrooms updates feel “bland, buggy, short” is a value signal, not a QA one — the promise of that game is discovery, and a safe on-cadence level satisfies the roadmap while quietly breaking the promise. Invisible in MAU, obvious in review text, and it compounds.

SCUM — their own insight, applied

If it’s publishing with no ownership, it’s the Star Wars problem in a different costume: reputation and cash flow, real capacity consumed, no asset created. And harder — a persistent-world survival sim would be the most operationally demanding thing in the building, indefinitely, during the exact window they need capacity for their own IP.

  • What’s the stake? Equity, participation, or fee-for-service? Does an acquirer inherit anything?
  • Who owns the player relationship across Gamepires, Splash Damage and Secret Mode? Who speaks, and who decides?
  • It arrives in deficit — 73% lifetime against 60% recent, peak 45k → 5.5k. Price the repair into the deal.
  • Your unfair advantage: you were at Jagex when Jagex owned Gamepires. Nobody else they can call has that.
Execution

Sequencing is the capacity plan.

Q4 load: a Star Wars launch on 6 Oct, two live roadmaps, a brand-new dev partner on Chained Together, a studio going 3 → 20, and a SCUM decision. That isn’t a headcount problem — it’s more simultaneous unmade decisions than any group of people can hold at once. Saying that back to them plainly is probably the most useful sentence of the day.

  • One big bet at a time. One IP in expansion; everything else in harvest. The discipline isn’t doing less — it’s never having two things that need the same scarce attention.
  • Sheffield’s ramp is the master schedule. Draw the plan against that curve, not against ambition. And the studio is itself an equity asset — known unit economics beat paying externals. Secker framed it as cost; it’s also an exit move, and they may not have said that out loud yet.
  • Stop rules agreed in advance. What kills or defers a plan — decided calm, not at 11pm in launch week.
  • Decision rights. Who commits Sheffield time, who arbitrates when two titles want the same sprint.
Ask
  1. What does Emona need to be true in three years? What story do they tell a buyer?
  2. Which IPs do you own outright, which are publishing rights, and what reverts when?If this takes more than a minute to answer, that’s your first engagement.
  3. What’s the most valuable thing you own that you’re doing nothing with?
  4. If you could only run one value-creation plan for twelve months — which IP, which move?
  5. What would a buyer discount you for today?
  6. What rights have you already granted that a buyer would want back?
  7. What does Sheffield get measured on in year one?If the answer is throughput or cadence, that’s your opening.
  8. When a drop ships, how do you know a month later whether it grew the asset or just fed the calendar?
Close with

01 IP value-creation plan

Rights position, asset condition, ranked moves per IP, sequenced against Sheffield’s real capacity curve, with the proof points each stage generates. A growth plan built on internal optimism gets discounted in diligence. One built on an independent read survives it.

02 Player Trust baselines

The evidence layer that makes the plan real, and valuable standing alone. Chained Together’s monetisation call, the Backrooms quality bar, and a SCUM pre-transition baseline — deal budget rather than operating budget.

03 The operating rhythm

Standing cadence: what’s happening with players, what changed, what to do. The mechanism that keeps a 20-person content team from optimising for the only thing it can measure.

Sheffield answers “can we ship it?”
Kritiq answers “should we, and did it land?”
The free, easy yes

Whiteboard their Galactic Racer launch triage in the room.

Signal tiers, named owners, escalation thresholds, and a written “what we will not do in week one” list. It costs an hour, it’s genuinely useful fifteen days out, and every later conversation starts from “that was useful” rather than from a deck.

Don’t
  • Drop back to live-ops. It’s the comfortable conversation and a tenth of the value — every thread comes back to what this does to the asset.
  • Arrive as a tool or a dashboard. They’re data-literate and they just hired engineers.
  • Say the games are dying. 88–90% is healthy: protect and compound, not rescue.
  • Be dismissive about Star Wars. Agree fast, name what it does buy, move on — nobody wants to hear their biggest launch doesn’t matter two weeks out.
  • Use RuneScape more than once. It’s now the case study rather than colour: two decades of compounding one IP is exactly what they’re trying to do.
Before you walk in: every figure here is from public secondary sources — Steam and the publisher’s own site were unreachable when this was compiled, so pull live review counts on your phone. And if discovery shows nobody actually owns the live titles’ direction, split between publishing, Sheffield and the external devs, say so. It’s the most valuable thing you could tell them, and the thing an insider can’t.
