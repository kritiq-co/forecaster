# Proposal strategy — internal, not for Secret Mode

Reasoning behind `05-proposal-draft.md`: pricing, politics, and what to leave out of v1.

---

## 1. The single biggest shift: this is written for Dan

Ed sponsors, Dan defines and approves. So the proposal is addressed to Dan with Ed named as sponsor — not the other way round. Practical consequences:

- **It must be easy for Dan to say yes to.** One approver, one number, no clauses that need a lawyer or a board. Anything that turns this into an Ed-and-Emona conversation delays an "ASAP" start by weeks.
- **It must make Dan look good**, not expose a gap on his watch. The draft frames the problem as a consequence of growth ("building faster than planning can keep up") rather than a failure of anyone's function. That's both true and politically necessary.
- **But Ed has to introduce it.** Dan owning it makes it happen; Ed introducing it makes it cross-functional. Ask for both — it's in "What I need from you".

## 2. The real risk: the Dan / Alison seam

Alison Fraser is Commercial Director, reports separately, is a BD lead, **and was not in the room.**

The pen you're picking up lies partly in her territory. If she experiences this as Dan annexing franchise planning via a consultant, the map dies on contact with Commercial — not loudly, just by never being used. And she has every structural reason to read it that way: she wasn't consulted, and the person hiring you is her peer, not her boss.

Handling:
- **Meet her in week one**, before the audit starts. Not a courtesy call — ask her what the franchise plan should contain, and mean it.
- Name her as a **co-author** in the proposal, which the draft does. Get that language in front of her early.
- Frame outputs as **company artefacts owned by the exec**, never "the marketing plan."
- If she's resistant, that's information for Ed, and it's a structural problem that predates you. Surface it early rather than working around it — working around it is how six months disappears.

Second, smaller seam: you'll be "helping Joe and Dan get clarity on their own objectives." That is org design wearing a friendly hat. Keep it framed as *role clarity falling out of the portfolio map* rather than a review of anyone's job, and let them reach the conclusions.

## 3. Pricing — structure recommended, number is yours

**Take the retainer, not the day rate.** Strongly.

- March 2027 will not respect a day count. On a day rate you either eat the overage or invoice for it in the worst possible month.
- A retainer reads as partnership; a day rate reads as a timesheet, and timesheets get scheduled around.
- It's one number for Dan to approve, and it's stable in his budget.
- It protects you from scope creep arriving as goodwill.

**Arithmetic**, so you can set the number:

| Avg days/week | Days/month | Days over 6 months |
|---|---|---|
| 2.0 | ~8.7 | ~52 |
| 2.5 | ~10.8 | ~65 |
| 3.0 | ~13.0 | ~78 |

At 2.5 days/week, a £1,200/day equivalent is ~£13k/month (~£78k total); £1,500/day is ~£16.2k/month (~£97k). **Treat those as arithmetic, not market data** — I have no verified UK rate benchmarks for fractional games exec work, and you know that market far better than I do. Set the number; the structure is the part I'd defend.

Two mechanics worth including:
- **"Up to X days per month"** in the retainer, with sustained overage handled at the month-three review rather than monthly. Removes the argument without giving away unlimited time.
- **Month-three review as a mutual checkpoint, not a break clause.** They need six. A review builds Dan's confidence in approving six without handing him an easy exit at three.

## 4. Equity — raise it, but not yet

Not discussed, and the draft leaves fees as a single line deliberately.

**The case for raising it now:** you'd be authoring the value-creation plan that drives their exit. Participation is most defensible at the point you start, and it aligns you with the outcome rather than the hours.

**The case against, which I think wins for v1:** Dan can approve a fee. Dan cannot approve equity — that's Ed and Emona, it takes weeks, and it converts a simple yes into a negotiation at exactly the moment they've said they need someone ASAP. You'd be trading a fast start for an option you can still get later.

**Recommendation:** fees only in v1. Raise participation at the month-three review or as a condition of anything beyond six months — by which point you'll have leverage you don't have today, because the map will exist and they'll be using it.

If you disagree, the version that doesn't slow things down is a small success fee tied to defined milestones rather than equity. Easier to approve, still aligns.

## 5. Title

Not discussed; you think you can define it. Recommendation: **something that implies ownership, not advice.**

"Advisor" gets listened to and ignored. "Consultant" gets scheduled around. You need people to treat your outputs as decisions-in-progress, not opinions.

**Interim Portfolio Director**, reporting to Dan, is honest about the temporary nature, carries enough authority to convene people, and doesn't threaten either Dan or Alison — it's explicitly a gap-filling role, which is exactly what it is. Avoid anything with "Chief" or "Head of" in it; it reads as a land grab to the two people whose cooperation you need most.

## 6. The Jan 2028 horizon is yours, and that has an implication

You set it, it landed, and fifteen months is a sound planning horizon — long enough to complete platform and territory moves and prove a playbook twice, short enough that people can hold it in their heads. Keep it.

But it's worth being clear with yourself that **it's a planning horizon, not necessarily the value horizon.** You still don't know Emona's intended hold period, and that's the one input that decides whether the Jan 2028 map is *the sale preparation* or *a milestone on the way to a 2029–30 sale*. The two produce genuinely different plans — a shorter hold pushes everything toward provable, bankable moves and away from anything that lands late; a longer one makes a sequel or a new IP rational.

Don't ask in the proposal. Ask Ed, privately, once you're engaged and the catalogue audit gives you a natural reason to — "to sequence this properly I need to know what we're optimising the timeline against." That's a legitimate question from the person holding the pen, and an odd one from a candidate.

A related trap the draft now guards against: **don't let anyone pin a revenue number to Jan 2028 before Phase 1.** With no analyst, no telemetry and possibly no per-title P&L, any figure agreed now is invented. Define the portfolio *state* first, attach financials once the audit gives you a base. Otherwise you spend month ten defending a number you made up in month one.

## 7. Alison — you have a warm start, use it before signature

You've met, and she knows you were coming in to talk about helping. That's better than cold: you can approach her without it being an ambush, and she already has a category to put you in.

The strongest play is to talk to her **before the engagement is agreed**, not in week one. Someone consulted while the scope is still being written is a shaper; someone met in week one is being managed. Same conversation, completely different standing — and it costs you nothing but an hour.

**Go through Dan, never around him.** Frame it as helping: *"Would it be useful if I spoke to Alison before we finalise scope, so this reflects what Commercial needs from it too?"* That makes Dan look inclusive, gives you the relationship with his blessing, and surfaces any Commercial-side objection while it's still cheap to design around rather than expensive to repair. The draft now offers this explicitly under "What I need from you."

If Dan resists that suggestion, note it. It would tell you something useful about how the seam is likely to behave once you're inside.

## 8. Still open

- **Fees.** The only blank in the draft.
- **Start date.**

## 9. On the month-six destination

Dan and Ed don't know what happens after six months; you sense a longer-term role until you make yourself redundant or they hire.

**Name the redundancy goal explicitly in the proposal** — the draft does, in Phase 3 and in "what good looks like."

It's counter-intuitive but it's the right commercial play as well as the honest one. Someone who arrives saying "my job is to make this unnecessary" is trusted faster, given access sooner, and — because trust and access are what determine whether six months actually works — is markedly more likely to be asked to stay. Trying to look indispensable from day one produces the opposite of both.

## 10. Things deliberately left out of v1

- Any mention of an exit, Emona's timeline, or enterprise value. **You know it's the real game; the proposal shouldn't say so.** Dan is approving a portfolio and programme engagement. The equity story is what the work produces, not what it advertises — and putting it on paper invites a conversation above Dan's head.
- Detailed per-title analysis. You have it, but it belongs in the first working session, not the proposal — leading with answers before you've been granted access looks like you've pre-judged their business.
- The SCUM and Squad deal-structure questions. Sharp, important, and premature in a document Alison may read before she's met you.
