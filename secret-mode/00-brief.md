# Secret Mode — half-day session prep

**22 Sep 2026 · Kritiq** — the real brief: *building a compelling value-creation plan for the IP Secret Mode actually owns, and being able to execute it without the team falling over.*

> **Sourcing note.** Figures below are from public secondary sources. `kritiq.co`, `wearesecretmode.com`, Steam and gg.deals were all blocked by this environment's egress proxy, so nothing came from a primary page. Directionally right, not quotable — verify before you use a number in the room.

---

## 1. The actual problem

Emona bought in March 2025. They will want to sell. To sell well, Secret Mode needs an **equity story**, and an equity story is not a slate — it's a defensible claim about assets a buyer gets to keep.

**Star Wars: Galactic Racer is not that asset.** It has to go well, and going well buys three real things: reputation, licensor relationships, and proof they can deliver a top-tier licensed launch. Those raise the *multiple* by making the team look capable. They do not raise the *asset base*, because when the licence ends the revenue ends with it and nothing transfers to the acquirer. A buyer capitalises owned IP and discounts licensed revenue heavily — sometimes to near zero on the terminal value.

So the question on the table tomorrow is the right one: **for the IP where Secret Mode has real ownership and control, what creates the most enterprise value, in what order, and can they actually run it?**

Sequel? Console? A partner for the East? All three are plausible. They are not equally valuable, and — crucially — they are not equally *provable* inside the window.

### The line to open with

> **Player trust is the leading indicator. Enterprise value is the lagging one.**

A buyer's diligence reads revenue, which tells them where the asset *has been*. Sentiment tells you where it's *going* — typically two to four quarters ahead. Which cuts both ways, and both ways are the pitch:

- **Defensively:** an IP whose trust curve is decaying is a depreciating asset, and it will price that way by the time it shows in the P&L. You want to see that early enough to fix it.
- **Offensively:** you can *evidence value creation before it lands in the numbers*. That is the single hardest thing to do in a sale process, and it is precisely what an independent read provides.

That connects Kritiq's actual product directly to Emona's actual objective. It is a much stronger position than live-ops advisory, and it's the frame for the whole day.

## 2. First: what do they actually own?

**The value plan cannot be written before the rights position is known, and it's likely nobody has laid it out on one page.** Offer to be the person who does.

For every IP, three questions:
1. **Depth of ownership** — full IP, or publishing rights, or a rev-share with the original dev? Perpetual or term? What reverts, and when?
2. **Encumbrances** — platform exclusivities, territory grants, engine or middleware terms, rev-shares that survive a sale, change-of-control clauses.
3. **Defensibility** — can someone else make this?

⚠️ **The Backrooms problem, which diligence will find.** "The Backrooms" is a shared creepypasta. There are multiple competing Backrooms games on Steam. Secret Mode owns *their execution* — the title, the levels, the brand, the community, the 46k reviews — but not the concept, and a buyer's IP counsel will ask exactly that question. This doesn't make it a bad asset; it makes it an asset whose moat is **audience and execution, not exclusivity**, which changes where you should invest. Do not build the most expensive bet on the least defensible IP.

Contrast with **Chained Together**: a specific, ownable game with a distinctive hook and 10M+ buyers. That's a stronger foundation for a franchise play, if the rights are clean.

And the question that matters most tomorrow: **on SCUM, what's the stake?**

## 3. SCUM — apply their own insight to it

Splash Damage acquired Gamepires from Jagex (Feb 2026), are supporting development, and have asked Secret Mode to publish.

**If that's a publishing deal with no ownership, it is the Star Wars problem again in a different costume** — reputation and cash flow, real capacity consumed, no asset created. Worse than Star Wars in one respect: Star Wars is a short, contained, premium launch. SCUM is a persistent-world survival sim — always-online servers, private-server ecosystem, cheating, wipes, an in-game economy, a mod community, and a hardcore base with years of accumulated grievance. It would be the most operationally demanding thing in the building, indefinitely, during the exact window they need capacity for their own IP.

That is not an argument against it. It's an argument that **the deal structure is the whole question**:
- Is there equity, an IP stake, or a long-term participation a buyer would capitalise?
- Is the term long enough that an acquirer inherits something real?
- Does it come with capability they keep — live-ops muscle on a persistent-world title is genuinely transferable and genuinely scarce?
- Or is it fee-for-service that consumes the studio ramp they need for Chained Together and Backrooms?

**And the asset is arriving in trust deficit.** ~6M copies, but **73% positive lifetime vs ~60% in the last 30 days**; peak ~45.3k concurrent (Jun '25) → ~5.5k. Players are unhappier *now* than historically, meaning it's ongoing rather than a bad patch already recovered from.

**Structural risk to name out loud:** Gamepires develops, Splash Damage supports, Secret Mode publishes. Three organisations, and unless someone has been explicit, **no single owner of the player relationship**. On a live title already in deficit, ambiguous ownership is the failure mode.

**Your unfair advantage.** You were at Jagex when Jagex owned Gamepires. You know the asset, the team and the real history from the inside. Nobody else Secret Mode can call has that. Be scrupulous about separating your own read from anything confidential — but the judgement is legitimately yours and it is worth a great deal here.

> That criticism is a value signal, not a QA signal. The promise of ETB is *discovery*. A short, safe, on-cadence level satisfies the roadmap and quietly breaks the promise, and each one moves a fan a notch toward "they're just filling the calendar now." It's invisible in MAU, obvious in review text, and it compounds — and it is exactly the drift a new in-house studio with a delivery mandate defaults into, because cadence is measurable and resonance isn't. **A content pipeline optimised for throughput will depreciate the asset it was built to grow.** That's an enterprise-value argument for an independent read, and it's the one that should land with Emona.

**Chained Together** — 10M+ copies at **$4.99**. Anegar Games stepped back; **Lab42 Games** is new on someone else's codebase, with the roadmap being written now. Known friction: chain physics in 4-player, difficulty cliff in the back half. Lab42 inherits the code but not the instinct for what the game *is* — and the −9 is worth understanding before they set a roadmap on top of it.

**The rest** — Still Wakes the Deep (+ *Siren's Rest*), A Little to the Left, Dear Esther, DeathSprint 66, Everything is Crab, NUTMEG!, A Storied Life: Tabitha. Premium, finite, well-reviewed. Worth one question: **is there a dormant asset in there?** *A Little to the Left* in particular is a cosy-puzzle brand with mobile, sequel and platform surface that a $5-co-op lens wouldn't naturally look at. Cheap to test, and buyers like a portfolio with more than two horses.

## 5. The sequencing framework — the core of the session

He asked: sequel, console, a publisher for the East — which order, and what drives most value? Give them a way to decide rather than an answer, then apply it live.

### The principle

**Sequence by evidence generated per pound spent, not by revenue per pound spent.**

They're building a plan a buyer will *believe*, not just cash. A buyer pays a premium for a **proven, repeatable playbook** and discounts an unproven roadmap. So the early moves should be cheap, fast, low-risk and *proof-generating* — because they're what make the expensive later moves fundable and credible.

### Score every move on five axes

| Axis | Question |
|---|---|
| **Value type** | One-off cash, recurring revenue, or a new asset? *(Recurring drives the multiple hardest — it's the difference between a P&L and an annuity.)* |
| **Evidence** | Does it prove the playbook repeats? |
| **Capacity cost** | Sheffield months, publishing attention, cash. |
| **Risk to the asset** | Can this damage the trust curve? |
| **Time to value** | Does it land *inside* the window, or after the buyer has already priced the business? |

### The ladder that falls out

**Tier 1 — Harvest what's already proven** *(0–9 months · low cost · near-zero asset risk)*
Console and platform expansion, territory deals, edition and pricing architecture. These monetise demand that already exists without touching the game or the player promise.

- **Console for Chained Together is the standout.** 10M+ owners at $5 on PC, and console audiences pay more for exactly this kind of couch/party co-op. It's the highest cash-per-unit-of-risk move available, and Sheffield was explicitly built to do ports.
- **The East is a Tier 1 move too** — a partner does the work, so capacity cost is low, and it proves the IP travels, which materially widens the buyer pool.
- ⚠️ **But mind the rights.** *Every territory or platform right you grant is a right the acquirer doesn't get.* A five-year exclusive China grant signed eighteen months before a sale can cost more in valuation than it earns in fees. Keep terms short, insist on reversion and change-of-control provisions, and treat every rights deal as a decision about the sale, because it is.

**Tier 2 — Prove recurring revenue** *(6–18 months · medium cost · this is the multiple)*
Paid content, cosmetics, seasonal cadence on whichever title supports it. This is what Sheffield exists for, and it's the single biggest driver of how a buyer values the business: recurring revenue with a known content cost per drop is an annuity; launch spikes are not.

- Chained Together is the obvious candidate — but it's also the **highest-stakes judgement call in the portfolio**, because every monetisation option renegotiates a deal 10 million people think closed at five dollars. Get it right and it's the most profitable thing they own. Get it wrong and the 90% becomes a 60% in a fortnight and doesn't come back. *That decision is worth de-risking with evidence, and the roadmap is being written right now.*

**Tier 3 — The big bet** *(18 months+ · high cost · high variance)*
A sequel. The only move that resets an IP's clock and turns a decaying asset into a franchise — and the one that eats the most capacity, takes the longest, and is most likely to land *after* the window.

- **The hard test: an announced-but-unshipped sequel is worth far less at exit than a shipped, growing live title.** Only commit if it can ship and show traction inside the window — or if the plan is explicitly to sell the pipeline, which is a different and riskier story.
- If a sequel happens, it should be on the most **defensibly owned** IP, funded by Tiers 1 and 2, and started only once one of them has proved the playbook.

### The counter-intuitive answer to give them

Most teams in this position reach for the sequel first, because it's the most exciting and feels the most like value creation. **For a 2–3 year exit window it's usually the wrong first move.** Ports and territories convert proven demand into cash and evidence in months; live content proves the annuity; the sequel is the thing those two earn you the right to fund. Run it the other way round and you spend the whole window on the highest-variance bet with nothing proved behind it.

## 6. Execution — "how do we do this without falling over"

This is not a separate problem. **Sequencing *is* the capacity plan.**

**Name the load out loud.** Into Q4 they're carrying: a licensed Star Wars launch on 6 Oct, two live titles needing roadmaps, a brand-new dev partner on Chained Together, a 3-person studio scaling to ~20, a SCUM decision, and the rest of the slate. That's not a headcount problem — it's **more simultaneous unmade decisions than any group of people can hold at once.** Saying that back to them plainly is probably the most useful sentence of the day.

Four things that actually stop a team falling over:

1. **One big bet at a time.** At any moment exactly one IP is in *expansion* (expensive, attention-heavy, senior-time-hungry); everything else is in *harvest* (systematic, pipeline-run, low decision load). The discipline isn't doing less — it's never having two things that need the same scarce attention.
2. **Sheffield's ramp is the master schedule.** 3 → ~20 over a year is the real constraint on everything in Tiers 1 and 2. The value plan should be drawn *against that curve*, not against ambition. Worth naming that the studio is itself an enterprise-value asset: an in-house capability with known unit economics ("we extend IP at £X per drop") supports a multiple in a way that paying externals never does. Dan Secker's stated rationale was cost; it's also an exit move, and they may not have framed it that way yet.
3. **Stop rules, agreed in advance.** What makes you kill or defer a plan? Decided when calm, not at 11pm in week one of a launch.
4. **Decision rights per workstream.** Who can commit Sheffield time? Who signs a territory deal? Most overwhelm in a small publisher is ambiguity about who decides, not volume of work.

## 7. Questions for the room

**The value plan**
1. What does Emona need to be true in three years for this to be a good outcome? What's the story they'd tell a buyer?
2. Which of your IPs do you own outright, which are publishing rights, and what reverts when? *(If this takes more than a minute to answer, that's your first engagement.)*
3. What's the most valuable thing you own that you're doing nothing with?
4. If you could only run one value-creation plan for the next twelve months, which IP and which move?
5. What would a buyer discount you for today?

**Sequencing**
6. Which of these have you already scoped — console for Chained Together, an eastern partner, a sequel? What did the numbers say?
7. What's your read on Backrooms' defensibility? Has anyone stress-tested the IP position?
8. What rights have you already granted that a buyer would want back?

**SCUM**
9. What's the stake — ownership, participation, or fee-for-service? Does an acquirer inherit anything?
10. Who owns the player relationship across the three parties? Who speaks, and who decides?
11. Do you know what the 60% is made of? *(A shrug here is the engagement.)*
12. What would you need to believe to walk away?

**Execution**
13. What does Sheffield get measured on in year one? *(Throughput or cadence = your opening.)*
14. Who can commit Sheffield's time, and who arbitrates when two titles want the same sprint?
15. When a content drop ships, how do you know a month later whether it grew the asset or just fed the calendar? What's the number, and who looks at it?
16. Who reads review *text*, not scores? How often? *(Usually lands hard.)*

**The launch, briefly — it's 15 days out**
17. What did you agree in advance that you will *not* respond to in week one?
18. How does licensor approval change your response time on a community issue? An unlicensed game hotfixes comms in an hour — have you pre-agreed an escalation path with Lucasfilm?

## 8. Where Kritiq fits

Pitch at the value level, not the ops level. Same work, an order of magnitude more budget, and a sponsor (Emona) with a reason to fund it.

### Offer 1 — IP value-creation plan *(the main event)*
An independent, evidence-based plan across the owned portfolio: rights position, asset condition, the ranked set of moves per IP, sequenced against Sheffield's actual capacity curve, with the proof points each stage generates.

Why independent matters: **a growth plan built on internal optimism gets discounted in diligence. One built on an external read on player demand and durability survives it.** That's the difference between a roadmap and an asset.

### Offer 2 — Player Trust baselines as the evidence layer
The diagnostic that makes Offer 1 real, and valuable standing alone. Per IP: what the demand actually is, how durable the tail is, where trust is eroding, what players would and wouldn't accept. Three immediate applications they can feel:
- **Chained Together monetisation** — de-risk the biggest call they'll make in 2027, before Lab42's roadmap sets in concrete.
- **Backrooms content quality bar** — is "bland, buggy, short" a vocal minority or the leading edge? Genuinely different answers; they can't currently tell.
- **SCUM pre-transition baseline** — what they'd be inheriting, what's fixable, what to write into the deal. Deal budget rather than operating budget, and a decision not yet made.

### Offer 3 — The operating rhythm
Standing cadence: what's happening with players, what changed, what to do. The judgement layer on top of Sheffield's delivery layer — and the mechanism that keeps a 20-person content team from optimising for the only thing it can measure.

**Sheffield answers "can we ship it?" — Kritiq answers "should we, and did it land?"**

### The immediate, free, easy yes
Galactic Racer is in 15 days. Build their launch triage with them on a whiteboard in the room: signal tiers, named owners, escalation thresholds, and a written *"what we will not do in week one"* list. It costs you an hour, it's genuinely useful, and every later conversation starts from "that was useful" rather than from a deck.

## 10. Traps

- **Don't drop back to live-ops.** It's the comfortable conversation and it's a tenth of the value. Every thread comes back to: what does this do to the asset?
- **Don't arrive as a tool or dashboard.** They're data-literate and they just hired engineers. Kritiq is independent judgement — the thing they cannot generate internally *precisely because* they're inside it.
- **Don't tell them their games are dying.** 88–90% is healthy. The frame is *protect and compound*, not rescue.
- **Don't be dismissive about Star Wars.** He's already right that it doesn't build equity — agree quickly, note what it *does* buy (credibility, licensor relationships, proof of delivery, and a stronger hand in every future negotiation), and move on. Nobody wants to hear their biggest launch doesn't matter two weeks out.
- **Use RuneScape once, but use it properly.** It's now directly on-point: two decades of compounding a single IP into $2B is the canonical version of what they're trying to do. That's not colour any more, it's the case study. Once, then talk about their games.
- **Watch for the organisational answer.** If nobody actually owns the live titles' direction — split between publishing, Sheffield and external devs — no amount of planning lands until that's named. It's the most valuable thing you could tell them and the thing an insider can't say.
