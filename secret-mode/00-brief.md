# Secret Mode — half-day session prep

**Session:** 22 Sep 2026 · **Topic:** how Kritiq supports Secret Mode's (semi) live-service titles + launch load

> **Sourcing note.** Everything below is from public secondary sources (news coverage, Steam aggregators). `kritiq.co`, `wearesecretmode.com` and `store.steampowered.com` were all blocked by this environment's egress proxy, so nothing is from a primary page. Treat the numbers as directionally right, not quotable — pull live Steam figures on your phone before you walk in. Confidence is marked per claim.

## 1. Who they are now (and why it matters)

Secret Mode in Sept 2026 is a materially different company to the one that published *Dear Esther* and *Still Wakes the Deep*.

| Change | Detail | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Independent | MBO Mar 2025 — James Schall + Ed Blincoe with Emona Capital, out of Sumo Group | High |
| Recognised | MCV/DEVELOP Indie Publisher of the Year 2025 | Medium |
| Became an **operator** | Took on *Escape the Backrooms* (Aug 2025, with Fancy Games + Blackbird) and *Chained Together* (Jun 2026, Anegar stepping back, **Lab42 Games** now developing) | High |
| Built the capacity | **Sheffield dev studio opened Aug 2026** — Dan Secker (VP Tech & Ops) + Jacob Habgood (technical development director). 3 staff → ~20 within a year. Remit: updates, DLC, ports, codebase maintenance, "specialised content updating pipelines" | High |
| Biggest launch ever, imminent | **Star Wars: Galactic Racer — 6 Oct 2026**, Fuse Games (ex-Criterion, Matt Webster), PS5/XSX/PC. Explicitly *not* season-pass live service: premium, campaign + arcade + multiplayer in the box | High |

**The one-sentence read:** they've acquired two accidental live-service businesses and are now building a studio to feed them — but they've built *capacity* (people who can ship content) ahead of the *operating system* (how you decide what content, for whom, and how you know it worked). That gap is exactly the thing Kritiq sells.

**And:** the licensed Star Wars launch is **15 days after your meeting.** The "how do we not get overwhelmed" question isn't hypothetical or future — it's the room's actual weather tomorrow. Do not treat it as the second agenda item.

## 2. Title-by-title read

### Escape the Backrooms — the content treadmill
- ~88% positive lifetime (~46k Steam reviews); ~86% last 30 days (~3.9k reviews). Still healthy. *(Medium — one aggregator reported 90%/162k, which doesn't reconcile; verify.)*
- All-time peak ~48.9k concurrent at 1.0 (23 Oct 2025). Now ~5k concurrent. That's a normal post-1.0 decay curve, not a crisis.
- Console: PS5/XSX 28 May 2026, Switch 2 7 Jul 2026, crossplay. Patch 1.4 27 Aug 2026 + hotfix 7 Sep.
- **The signal that matters:** a recurring community criticism that recent updates ship *"bland, buggy, short levels"* — one update reportedly ~10 minutes of content. *(Medium confidence, but this is the thread to pull.)*

> **Why this is a trust problem, not a content problem.** The player promise for ETB is *discovery* — a new level should feel like a place you found. A short, safe, on-cadence level technically satisfies the roadmap and quietly breaks the promise. Each one moves a fan one notch toward "they're just filling the calendar now." That's invisible in MAU and obvious in review text, and it compounds. It is also precisely the failure mode a new in-house content studio with a delivery mandate will drift into by default, because cadence is measurable and resonance isn't.

### Chained Together — the highest-stakes judgement call in the portfolio
- 10M+ copies at **$4.99**. ~90% positive lifetime (~20.5k reviews); ~81% last 30 days (~760). Slight recent softening.
- Anegar Games stepped back; **Lab42 Games** is a brand-new team on someone else's codebase. Roadmap "coming months" — i.e. being written right now, probably this quarter.
- Known friction: chain physics bugs in 4-player, difficulty cliff in the back half.
- **The question nobody has answered yet:** what is the business model of a 10-million-unit $5 game in year three? Everything they might do — cosmetics, paid content, a sequel, a price rise, seasons — is a renegotiation of a deal players think they already closed at five dollars. Get it right and it's the most profitable thing in the portfolio. Get it wrong and the 90% becomes a 60% in a fortnight, and it won't come back.
- A new dev team is the riskiest possible moment to make that call, because Lab42 inherits the code but not the instinct for what the game *is*.

### SCUM — ⚠️ check the premise
SCUM appears **not** to be Secret Mode's. Public record: Gamepires developed/published it, Jagex acquired Gamepires, and **Splash Damage acquired Gamepires from Jagex in Feb 2026**. If Secret Mode has a connection, it isn't public.

Two possibilities: (a) you were drawing on it as a comparable from your Jagex years, or (b) there's something unannounced. Worth a quiet check before you use it as a "your titles" example in the room — getting a portfolio wrong in the first ten minutes is expensive.

**Either way it's a superb teaching case**, and you own the story first-hand:
- ~6M copies. **73% lifetime (38k reviews) but ~60% last 30 days (546 reviews).**
- Peak ~45.3k concurrent (Jun 2025) → ~5.5k. Down ~88% from peak.

That gap between lifetime and recent is the entire Kritiq thesis rendered as two numbers: the asset is still enormous, and the relationship is degrading faster than the revenue is. Lifetime score is a memory; the 30-day score is the current state of the marriage. Most studios manage the memory.

### The rest
Still Wakes the Deep (+ *Siren's Rest* DLC), A Little to the Left, Dear Esther, DeathSprint 66, Everything is Crab (May 2026, Odd Dreams Digital), NUTMEG!, A Storied Life: Tabitha. Premium, finite, well-reviewed. Not the topic — but useful context for *why* the live titles feel foreign to the team: most of Secret Mode's institutional muscle is in launching things that end, and live titles never end.

## 3. The two problems in the room (and they're the same problem)

**Stated problem A — "what should we be doing with our live titles?"**
They have the pipeline (Sheffield) and the content. What they lack is a defensible answer to *what to build next and why*, that isn't "the loudest Discord voice" or "what the roadmap said in March."

**Stated problem B — "how do we not get overwhelmed on future launches?"**
Overwhelm in a small publishing team is almost never a headcount problem. It's a **decision problem**: at launch every signal arrives at once, undifferentiated and unranked, and everything feels equally urgent because nobody agreed in advance what *wasn't*. The team burns out triaging noise and the two things that actually mattered get handled late.

**These are one problem.** Both are "we can't tell which signals deserve a response." Say that out loud early — it reframes the half day from two shopping lists into one capability, and that capability is what Kritiq is.

## 4. Questions to ask (the actual value of the session)

Ask these; don't present at them. A half day where they talk for 60% of it is a half day that ends in a scope.

**Discovery — how decisions actually get made**
1. Walk me through the last content decision on Escape the Backrooms. Who proposed it, what evidence did they have, who could have said no?
2. When a patch ships, how do you know a week later whether it worked? What's the number, and who looks at it?
3. What are you *not* doing on these titles that you know you should be?
4. Where do the two titles disagree with each other about what "good" looks like?

**The trust question**
5. What do players think they bought? (Ask separately for ETB and Chained Together — the answers are very different, and the whole strategy follows from them.)
6. What's the thing you could do that would make your most loyal players feel taken for granted?
7. Who in the building reads review text, not review scores? How often? *(This usually lands hard.)*

**Chained Together specifically**
8. What's the monetisation thesis for a $5 game with 10 million owners — and is it written down anywhere?
9. How does Lab42 learn what the game *is*, not just how the code works? What's the handover of intent, as opposed to the handover of repos?

**Sheffield / capacity**
10. What does the Sheffield studio get measured on in year one? *(If the answer is throughput or cadence, that's your opening — that measure will manufacture the "bland short levels" problem at scale.)*
11. At ~20 people, who decides what they work on — the studio, the publisher, or the community?

**Launch load — get concrete, Galactic Racer is in 15 days**
12. For 6 Oct: who is on point for community signal, and what are they empowered to decide without escalating?
13. What did you agree in advance you will *not* respond to in week one?
14. What's the trigger that makes you change the plan mid-launch — and who's allowed to pull it?
15. Post-mortem on your last launch: what did the team say broke them? *(Ask for the human answer, not the process answer.)*
16. Licensor dimension: how does Lucasfilm/Disney approval change your response time on a community issue? *(This is a real and under-modelled constraint — an unlicensed game can hotfix a comms message in an hour; a licensed one may not. If they haven't pre-agreed an escalation path with the licensor, that's a concrete, valuable thing you can flag tomorrow.)*

## 5. Where Kritiq plugs in — three offers, deliberately sized

Lead with the smallest. A half day should end with one thing they can say yes to this week, not a menu.

### Offer 1 — Launch readiness, Galactic Racer *(days, not weeks — do it now)*
The only offer with a deadline attached, which makes it the easiest yes in the room.
- Pre-agreed **signal triage**: what counts as noise, what's a watch item, what's a stop-the-line issue — decided *before* launch, when nobody is tired.
- **Named owners and decision rights** per signal class, so nothing waits on a group chat at 11pm.
- **Escalation thresholds** written down, licensor path included.
- A short, honest **"what we will not do in week one"** list — the single most effective anti-overwhelm artefact there is, because it gives people permission to not act.

The pitch: *this doesn't add work before launch, it removes decisions from the week you'll have least capacity to make them.*

### Offer 2 — Player Trust health check on the two live titles *(weeks)*
An independent read on where ETB and Chained Together actually stand with their players — what the relationship is, where it's quietly eroding, and what to protect. Deliverable is a prioritised view of what to do next and what not to touch.

Two specific hooks you can name in the room:
- ETB's "bland, buggy, short levels" sentiment — is it a vocal minority or the leading edge of the curve? That's a genuinely different answer and they can't currently tell.
- Chained Together's monetisation decision — the health check is the input that de-risks the biggest call they'll make in 2027, before Lab42's roadmap sets in concrete.

### Offer 3 — The operating rhythm *(ongoing, the real prize)*
Standing cadence: what's happening with the players, what changed, what to do about it. Effectively the judgement layer on top of the Sheffield delivery layer.

Frame it as the thing that keeps the studio from becoming a content treadmill: **Sheffield answers "can we ship it?" — Kritiq answers "should we, and did it land?"** A 20-person internal team with no external read on trust will optimise for the thing it can measure. You're the counterweight. That's a durable, budgetable role, and it's the one that compounds.

| Time | Block | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:20 | Framing, not pitching | Your Jagex/RuneScape angle in 5 min max, then the reframe: both their problems are one problem — signal triage. Set the goal: leave with one decision. |
| 0:20–1:20 | **Discovery — they talk** | Section 4, questions 1–7. Resist solving anything. Take verbatim notes on review-text answers. |
| 1:20–2:00 | Title deep dive: Chained Together | Q8–9. The monetisation thesis. Highest-value hour of the day; the decision is live and unmade. |
| 2:00–2:15 | Break | |
| 2:15–2:50 | Title deep dive: Escape the Backrooms + Sheffield | Q10–11. Content quality bar vs cadence. What Sheffield gets measured on. |
| 2:50–3:30 | **Launch load — Galactic Racer** | Q12–16. Go concrete. Whiteboard the triage tiers and the "will not do" list live, in the room. This block should *produce an artefact they keep*. |
| 3:30–4:00 | Close | Reflect back what you heard. Name the one thing you'd do first. Offer 1 as the immediate yes; 2 and 3 as the shape after. Agree next contact. |

**The move:** build the Galactic Racer triage list *with them, on a whiteboard, in the room*. They leave having received something real and unbilled, and they've experienced what working with you is like rather than hearing about it. Every subsequent conversation starts from "that was useful" instead of from a deck.

## 7. Traps

- **Don't arrive as a tool.** They're a data-literate publisher and they've just hired engineers. The moment this looks like a dashboard you're competing with their own roadmap. Kritiq is judgement — an independent read they cannot generate internally precisely *because* they're inside it.
- **Don't be the consultant who tells them their game is dying.** ETB at 88% and Chained Together at 90% are healthy. The pitch is *protect this*, not *rescue this*. Decay-curve doom framing gets you politely shown out.
- **Don't undersell the launch block by putting it last on the clock.** It's their live pain, 15 days out. Give it a real slot and make it concrete.
- **Do check the SCUM premise** before you use it as one of "their" titles.
- **Don't over-index on RuneScape.** It's your credibility, not your content — two decades of a subscription MMO is a different animal to a $5 co-op ragebait platformer, and they'll know it. Use it once, for the trust mechanic, then talk about *their* games.
- **Watch for the real blocker being organisational.** If discovery reveals that nobody actually owns the live titles' direction — that it's split between publishing, the new studio, and the external devs — then no amount of insight lands until that's named. If you spot it, say it kindly and directly. It's the most valuable thing you could tell them, and it's the thing an insider can't say.
