# Secret Mode — in-room card

## Open with
> **Player trust is the leading indicator. Enterprise value is the lagging one.**
> Diligence reads revenue — where the asset has been. Sentiment tells you where it's going, two to four quarters ahead. So you can see erosion before it prices in, and evidence value creation before it lands in the P&L. That second one is the hardest thing to do in a sale process.

## The frame
Star Wars has to go well, and going well buys reputation, licensor relationships and proof of delivery — all of which lift the *multiple*. It doesn't lift the *asset base*: when the licence ends the revenue ends and nothing transfers. So the question is the owned IP: what creates the most value, in what order, and can they run it?

## The principle
**Sequence by evidence generated per pound spent, not revenue per pound spent.** A buyer pays a premium for a proven repeatable playbook and discounts an unproven roadmap.

**Score every move:** value type (one-off / recurring / new asset) · evidence generated · capacity cost · risk to the trust curve · does it land inside the window?

## The ladder
1. **Harvest** *(0–9mo, low cost, near-zero risk)* — console + platform, territory partners, edition/pricing. **Console for Chained Together is the standout**: 10M+ owners at $5 on PC, console pays more, Sheffield was built for ports. ⚠️ Every right you grant is a right the buyer doesn't get — short terms, reversion, change-of-control.
2. **Prove recurring** *(6–18mo — this is the multiple)* — content, cosmetics, seasons. Recurring revenue with known content cost is an annuity; launch spikes aren't. Chained Together is the candidate *and* the biggest risk: every option renegotiates a deal 10M people think closed at $5.
3. **The big bet** *(18mo+)* — a sequel. Only move that resets the clock. **An announced-but-unshipped sequel is worth far less at exit than a shipped, growing live title.** Must ship inside the window, on the most defensibly owned IP, funded by 1 and 2.

**The counter-intuitive bit:** for a 2–3 year window the sequel is usually the wrong *first* move. Ports and territories convert proven demand into cash and evidence in months. The sequel is what those earn you the right to fund.

## Two things diligence will find
- **Backrooms defensibility.** "The Backrooms" is shared creepypasta with competing games on Steam. They own their execution, brand, community and 46k reviews — not the concept. Moat is audience and execution, not exclusivity. *Don't put the most expensive bet on the least defensible IP.*
- **A throughput-measured content studio depreciates the asset it was built to grow.** The live gripe that Backrooms updates are "bland, buggy, short" is a value signal, not a QA one.

## SCUM — apply their own insight
If it's publishing with no ownership, it's the Star Wars problem in a different costume — reputation and cash flow, real capacity consumed, no asset created. And harder: a persistent-world sim is the most demanding thing in the building, indefinitely, during the window they need capacity for their own IP.
- **What's the stake?** Equity, participation, or fee-for-service? Does an acquirer inherit anything?
- **Who owns the player relationship** across Gamepires / Splash Damage / Secret Mode? Who speaks, who decides?
- Arriving in deficit: 73% lifetime vs 60% recent, peak 45k → 5.5k.
- You were at Jagex when Jagex owned Gamepires. Nobody else they can call has that.

## Execution — sequencing IS the capacity plan
Q4 load: Star Wars 6 Oct · two live roadmaps · new dev partner on Chained · studio 3→20 · SCUM decision. Not a headcount problem — **more simultaneous unmade decisions than a group of people can hold.**
1. **One big bet at a time** — one IP in expansion, everything else in harvest.
2. **Sheffield's ramp is the master schedule.** Draw the plan against the curve, not against ambition. *(And the studio is itself an EV asset: known unit economics beat paying externals. Secker framed it as cost — it's also an exit move.)*
3. **Stop rules agreed in advance.**
4. **Decision rights** — who commits Sheffield time, who arbitrates.

## Ask
1. What does Emona need to be true in three years? What story do they tell a buyer?
2. Which IPs do you own outright vs publishing rights, and what reverts when? *(>1 min to answer = first engagement)*
3. What's the most valuable thing you own that you're doing nothing with?
4. If you could only run one plan for twelve months — which IP, which move?
5. What would a buyer discount you for today?
6. What rights have you already granted that a buyer would want back?
7. What does Sheffield get measured on in year one? *(throughput = your opening)*
8. When a drop ships, how do you know a month later whether it grew the asset or fed the calendar?

## Close
1. **IP value-creation plan** — rights, asset condition, ranked moves, sequenced against Sheffield's capacity, with the proof points each stage generates. *A growth plan built on internal optimism gets discounted in diligence. One built on an external read survives it.*
2. **Player Trust baselines** — the evidence layer. Chained monetisation, Backrooms quality bar, SCUM pre-transition.
3. **Operating rhythm** — standing cadence.

**Sheffield answers "can we ship it?" — Kritiq answers "should we, and did it land?"**

**Free, easy yes:** whiteboard their Galactic Racer launch triage in the room. Signal tiers, owners, thresholds, a written "what we will not do in week one".

## Don't
- Drop back to live-ops. It's the comfortable conversation and a tenth of the value.
- Arrive as a tool. They just hired engineers.
- Say the games are dying. 88–90% is healthy — protect and compound, not rescue.
- Be dismissive about Star Wars. Agree fast, name what it *does* buy, move on.
- Use RuneScape more than once — but it's now the case study, not colour.
